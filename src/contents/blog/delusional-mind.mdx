## The one sentence I actually live by

> Overconfidence is always better than underconfidence, as long as you strive
> to make sure that overconfidence becomes the new baseline.

Read that second half again, because it is the whole thing. Not "be
overconfident." Anyone can walk around loud and empty. The trick is that you
start overconfident on purpose, and then you go and do the work that makes the
overconfidence *true*, until the level you were faking quietly becomes the floor
you stand on. Then you fake the next level. Repeat.

Underconfidence does the opposite. It waits for permission. It grinds the same
thing everyone else grinds so that, at best, it ends up exactly as good as
everyone else. **Underconfidence is a very polite way of guaranteeing you stay
average.**

My sister and brother-in-law spent years telling me to be more like everyone
else. "Do LeetCode. Apply like a normal person. Stop being a fool." They loved
me and they were describing the safe path. I kept choosing the delusional one.

This is the story of the delusional one working 8 times.

## The kid who was buying the whole company

There is a story I read once that I have never been able to shake.

A millionaire asks a sharp, broke kid straight out of high school how long it
will take him to buy a BMW. The kid thinks and says, maybe 20 or 30 years. The
millionaire is stunned. 30 years? You will have a job in 3 or 4, you could buy
one within another 5. Why so long?

And the kid says: you never said "a BMW *car*." So I assumed you meant the
company.

It sounds like a joke. It gives me goosebumps every time. Because that gap right
there, between "buy a car" and "buy the company," is the entire difference
between the 2 mindsets. Same words, same facts. One brain defaults to the
small reading, the other defaults to the enormous one and then spends its life
trying to close the distance.

> Overconfidence is just choosing the enormous reading by default.

I default to the enormous reading. It has cost me, and I would not trade it.
Here is the receipt.

## Where the delusion started (India, flukes 1 and 2)

**Fluke #1 was FairShare.** In my 2nd year of my undergrad degree, I cold reached to the
CEO of an IT startup
[Aniruddha Pimpalkhare](https://www.linkedin.com/in/aniruddhapimpalkhare/), with a pitch: to help him build an end-to-end
e-commerce app for the street vendors selling vegetables and fruits. He agreed in
the first meeting. I interned there for 4 months and shipped it. Nobody asked me
to invert a binary tree.

**Fluke #2 was StoneX**, and it is the one worth telling in full.

On-campus hiring in India works differently than in the US. Companies come to
campus for a day or more, run interview rounds after rounds back to back, and hire on the spot.
StoneX was one of them. The screening round was LeetCode, plus MySQL, plus an
aptitude and IQ section. I left the entire LeetCode section blank on principle -
I only did the 2 parts I actually believed measured something - and I passed
anyway.

It was 7pm and I was already halfway home when a friend called and told me I had
cleared into the next rounds. I was not interested. She made me come back to campus ASAP.

Of 500+ students, 30 were left. 8 would be hired. I was number 30 in the
line, about to do the **first in-person interview of my entire life.**

So I did the only rational thing: I interrogated all 29 people ahead of me as
they walked out. One got a brutal graph problem. One got dynamic programming.
One got asked networking and hardware questions for a software role. One got
grilled on operating-system internals. And there I was, completely unprepared,
because I had never actually wanted this. The only thing I had was this: I was
insanely confident in React and blockchain. I was the president of the
Blockchain Club at Symbiosis, and at that moment I believed I was a better React
dev than anyone in that hallway.

My turn. The staff engineer tells me to sit. I stay standing, and I say
something like:

> Sir, with respect, let me save us both the hour. I am bad at LeetCode and I
> don't believe it measures what you actually need built. I am an amazing
> React and blockchain dev. If
> that's what you're hiring, I'd like to sit and continue. If it isn't, I would not wanna waste your
> time.

He laughs and says, please, sit down.

For the next hour - not the scheduled 35 minutes, the full hour - it is React
design problems, live coding, and blockchain concepts. And I am *killing it.*

His last question was a trap. He offers me a choice: 1 final hard question, or 10
easy ones where a single wrong answer ends the interview. I was exhausted and I
wanted to go home quickly, so I said hit me with the hard one. He leans back and asks:
"Chicken or egg. Which came first?"

I say "Chicken." Instantly. No thinking.

He says, "Can you explain?"

I laugh hard and say, "You said *1* hard question, right?"

He laughed so hard he sent me straight to the final round.

The final round was with [Prashant
Sangoi](https://www.linkedin.com/in/prashantsangoi/), who runs StoneX's entire
Pune operation. He said he'd heard my interview went nothing like the others.
Then he asked me exactly one question: walk me through your whole life and how
you made your decisions. And so I did. He hired me on the spot. His words were roughly,
"I still have to decide on the other 7 or 8. But I want you." I accepted the offer right there.

StoneX is the only big company I have ever properly worked at. I interned there for 7
months. I was supposed to join full-time, but instead I left for my master's in
the US. Working at StoneX showed me how companies operate on a huge scale and helped me realise: **big companies move slow, and I will choose working at a startup any day of the
week.**

## Then the doubt showed up (USA, flukes 3 through 8)

I landed in the US and everyone around me was grinding LeetCode for interviews.
Every single person. And I was not.

So the doubt crept in, the exact doubt my sister and brother-in-law had been
warning me about. What if India was a fluke? What if FairShare and StoneX only
worked because of some local magic that does not exist here? What if they
were right and I was the idiot?

There was only one way to find out. Run the experiment again. And again. And
again.

**Fluke #3 - Spashta AI, June 2024.** My first US internship. I cold-outreached
[Prashant Serai](https://www.linkedin.com/in/prashantserai/), ex-Meta,
ex-Google, a PhD in voice tech, who was building SpashtaAI on noise reduction
and accent and language conversion. 2-3 calls with him. Zero LeetCode. I got in.
2 months later he wound the startup down. My first real swing in a brand new
country, and it connected. Luck, obviously. I was grinning like an idiot. Load
up the next one.

**Fluke #4 - Dextego, September 2024.** I cold-emailed
[Ioanna Mantzouridou Onasi](https://www.linkedin.com/in/joannemantzouridou/),
the CEO, a full slide deck on what Dextego could be doing better. She was
impressed enough to hop on a call and offer me an internship. But we both couldn't
agree on the logistics of the offer so I couldn't work there. Didn't matter, the
swing connected again. That is 2 in a row. Still just luck, right? Sure. Let's
keep testing that theory.

**Fluke #5 - SpatiumLab, April 2025.** This one is my favorite. I had started
learning Turkish, purely because I was obsessed with a Turkish drama called
*Bambaşka Biri*. Then I came across [Cem Ozen](https://www.linkedin.com/in/cemalper/),
a Turkish CEO in Bellevue running SpatiumLab. I cold-reached him (in
English, obviously) and got an interview. I spent the first 2 minutes
introducing myself entirely in Turkish. He cracked up. Then came an in-person
React and FastAPI round, which I aced. 3-month internship. I got a job by opening
in a language I was learning off a soap opera. Tell me that is normal. I could
not stop laughing.

**Fluke #6 - Abby, July to November 2025.** I had just built an AI Therapist at the
UWB Hacks AI hackathon (my 2nd time competing) and lost. Then I found Abby AI,
an AI therapy app, used it, and immediately saw a dozen things I would change. So
I cold-outbounded the CEO,
[Julian Sarokin](https://www.linkedin.com/in/juliansarokin/), 3 times with a
deck. He said yes. I started with a 1-month internship that converted into a full-time
offer lined up exactly with my graduation. I was there 5 months. And now one of
these "flukes" was paying me like it meant it.

**Fluke #7 - Alex AI.** Around this time, the founding-engineer role I had lost a
year earlier to a visa wall came back around, because I never stopped following
up. That whole saga - the lost offer, the 17 emails, the day John finally
replied - is its own story, and I already told it in
[The Wrong Competition](/blogs/the-wrong-competition). Short version: it worked,
and I moved to SF. My so-called flukes were paying rent in San Francisco now.

## Fluke #8

I left Alex in May 2026.

My sister and brother-in-law, right on cue: "Okay. *Now* will you just do
LeetCode and apply like everyone else?"

I laughed and bet on my stupid delusional mind an 8th time.

A UW alum named Jessica connected me to [Mek](https://www.linkedin.com/in/srunyu/),
then the CTO of Stuut AI. 5 in-person rounds. Zero LeetCode. Hired in 5 days.
I am there right now, owning the voice-agents work, and happier than I have been
anywhere.

8 for 8.

## What the tally actually says

From FairShare to Stuut, that is 8 - 1 big company and a long string of
startups. And I have still never passed a LeetCode-style interview, because I
almost never had to sit one.

I am not smug about it and I am not telling you to skip the grind. If LeetCode is
the door to where you want to go, go grind it, and I will cheer for you. The
point was never that the door is fake.

Here is what I actually mean by "fluke." Every time one of these worked, a voice -
sometimes my family's, mostly my own - said the same word: lucky. A one-off.
Don't get cocky. And I understand why. A fluke is a single event, so calling it
that let me enjoy the win without having to defend the belief underneath it. It
kept me humble and it kept me small in the exact same breath. But a fluke that
repeats stops being a fluke. By the 3rd or 4th time, "fluke" was just the story
my doubt told itself so it would never have to say the scarier thing out loud:
this is working, and I am the reason.

And the delusional mind is just the part of me that keeps betting before the
proof shows up. I call it delusional because that is exactly how it feels from
the inside - deciding you will pull it off while every reasonable person, half my
own family included, is telling you to be realistic. I keep it because the
realistic version of me would have ground LeetCode, blended into the crowd, and
disappeared.

> Being delusional is only embarrassing right up until it works. After that,
> everyone just calls it vision.

Underconfidence would have had me in the same room as everyone else, grinding the
same problems, coming out indistinguishable. Overconfidence put me in rooms I had
no business walking into - and then, every single time, I did the work to make it
so I did belong there. That is the sentence from the top, running live: start
above your level, then drag your level up to meet it, until it's just the
baseline. Then do it again.

And the honest, slightly petty truth?

**There has been no greater joy on this earth than proving my sister and
brother-in-law wrong, 8 times and counting.** I love them for doubting me.
They gave me something to disprove.

I am going to keep betting on the delusional mind. Every single day. Any day.
